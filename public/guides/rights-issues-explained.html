Rights issues on the CSE explained: subscribe, sell or let lapse

A rights issue is a way for a listed company to raise new money from its existing shareholders. You are offered the right, but not the obligation, to buy new shares in proportion to what you already own, usually at a price below the current market price. You can take them up, sell the right to someone else, or let it lapse.

Not investment advice. Every rights issue has its own circular with exact dates and terms. Read it, and ask your broker if anything is unclear.

The key terms

TermMeaning
RatioHow many new shares you can buy per shares held, e.g. "1 for 5" means one new share for every five you own.
Rights price (issue price)The price you pay for each new share.
XR dateFrom this date the share trades without the right; buyers on or after it do not get rights.
Provisional Letter of Allotment (PAL)The document showing your entitlement. Rights are credited to your CDS account.
Rights trading periodA short window when the rights themselves trade on the CSE as a separate security.
Last date of renunciationThe final day to sell or transfer your rights.
Last date of acceptance and paymentThe deadline to pay for the new shares you want.

Typical sequence: shareholder approval and CSE approval, the XR date, PALs made available, rights trading starting a few market days later (circulars often say the fourth market day after the PAL is dispatched), the last date of renunciation, and finally the last date for acceptance and payment, followed by allotment and listing of the new shares. For example, one 2026 rights issue had rights trading start on 3 February, renunciation close on 11 February and acceptance and payment close on 16 February.

Your three choices

1. Subscribe (take up the rights)

You pay the rights price for the new shares by the acceptance deadline, following the payment method in the circular (for example through your bank or broker). You keep your percentage of the company. Your total investment rises.

2. Sell (renounce) the rights

During the rights trading period you can sell your rights on the CSE through your broker, like a share. You receive cash and the buyer gets the right to subscribe. Normal transaction costs apply.

3. Let them lapse

If you do nothing, the rights expire. You receive nothing, and your share of the company is diluted. Doing nothing is a decision too, and usually the most expensive one when rights have value.

Theoretical ex-rights price (TERP)

Because new shares are sold below the market price, the share price usually adjusts on the XR date. The theoretical ex-rights price estimates where it "should" settle:

TERP = (N × current price + R × rights price) ÷ (N + R), where you get R new shares for every N held.

Example: a share trades at Rs 60. The company offers 1 new share for every 4 held (N = 4, R = 1) at Rs 40.

  • TERP = (4 × 60 + 1 × 40) ÷ 5 = 280 ÷ 5 = Rs 56
  • Theoretical value of one right = TERP − rights price = 56 − 40 = Rs 16

The market price will not match TERP exactly; it depends on how investors judge the reason for raising money.

Worked example for a shareholder

You own 2,000 shares bought at an average cost (including fees) of Rs 50.56. The 1-for-4 issue above gives you 500 rights.

  • Subscribe: pay 500 × 40 = Rs 20,000. You now own 2,500 shares. Total cost = 2,000 × 50.56 + 20,000 = Rs 121,120, so your new average cost is Rs 48.45.
  • Sell the rights at, say, Rs 14 each: receive 500 × 14 = Rs 7,000 before fees. Keep 2,000 shares.
  • Let them lapse: keep 2,000 shares, receive nothing.

Questions worth asking

  • Why does the company need the money: growth, paying down debt, or covering losses?
  • Do the major shareholders plan to subscribe?
  • Are warrants or other securities attached to the rights?
  • Can you afford to subscribe without selling other investments you would rather keep?

Record-keeping

If you subscribe, add the new shares and the cash paid to your record. If you sell rights, note the proceeds. CSE Portfolio lets you add the new shares as a purchase at the rights price, which updates your average cost and break-even price automatically.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if I do nothing?

Your rights lapse after the acceptance deadline. You receive nothing for them, and your percentage ownership of the company falls because new shares are issued to others. If the rights had a market value, doing nothing means giving that value up.

Can I apply for more shares than my entitlement?

Some rights issues allow applications for additional shares that other shareholders did not take up. Whether this is allowed, and how extra shares are allocated, is stated in the circular.

Why do rights trade below the theoretical value?

Rights trade for a short time, often with limited buyers, so their price can be below the theoretical value. Transaction costs also apply to trading rights.
