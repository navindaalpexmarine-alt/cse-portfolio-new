ASPI vs S&P SL20: the two main CSE indices explained

The Colombo Stock Exchange (CSE) has two headline indices. The All Share Price Index (ASPI) tracks every listed ordinary share. The S&P Sri Lanka 20 (S&P SL20) tracks 20 large, frequently traded companies. Most days they move together, but not always, and the differences tell you something.

Not investment advice. Index levels describe the past. They do not predict where prices go next.

What an index is

An index is a single number that summarises the prices of many shares. If the index rises 1%, the combined value of the shares in it rose about 1% that day. It lets you judge "how did the market do?" without looking at hundreds of prices.

The ASPI

  • Covers: all listed ordinary voting and non-voting shares.
  • Weighting: by market capitalisation (share price × shares in issue). Bigger companies count more.
  • Base: 100 in 1985. An ASPI of 21,000 means the market value of listed shares (adjusted for new listings, rights issues and similar changes) is about 210 times its 1985 level in rupee terms.
  • Strength: the broadest picture of the whole market.
  • Weakness: it includes shares that rarely trade and companies with a small free float, so its daily moves can be pushed by a handful of large or thinly traded names.

The S&P SL20

  • Covers: 20 of the largest and most liquid companies, chosen by S&P Dow Jones Indices together with the CSE using size and trading-activity rules.
  • Weighting: by float-adjusted market capitalisation, with caps so that no single company dominates.
  • Reviewed: periodically, when companies can be added or removed.
  • Strength: represents shares that institutions and foreign investors can actually buy and sell in size. It is also the index used for the CSE's market-wide circuit breakers.
  • Weakness: says little about small and mid-sized companies.

When they disagree

What you seeWhat it often means
ASPI up, S&P SL20 downSmaller or less-traded shares rose while some big liquid names fell. Often a sign of strong local retail activity.
ASPI down, S&P SL20 upLarge blue chips held up or rose while the wider market weakened.
Both up strongly on high turnoverBroad buying across the market.
Index up but more shares fell than roseA few heavyweights carried the index. Check market breadth.

Other numbers to read with the index

  • Turnover: the rupee value of shares traded in the session. A move on high turnover carries more weight than the same move on thin trading.
  • Breadth: how many shares closed higher versus lower.
  • Foreign net buying or selling: published by the CSE; shows whether overseas investors were net buyers.
  • Market capitalisation: the total value of all listed shares.

Reading index changes sensibly

Daily moves of 0.5% to 1% are common on the CSE. Over months, the index is affected by interest rates (when deposit rates fall, shares often become more attractive), the economy, company profits, and confidence. A single day rarely means much on its own. Looking at weekly or monthly changes, and comparing with turnover, gives a better sense of direction.

Using the index to judge your own results

Compare your portfolio's percentage change with the ASPI over the same period. If you consistently lag the index, it is worth asking whether the time and fees spent on picking shares are adding value. CSE Portfolio's Insights tab shows this comparison for your holdings.

Frequently asked questions

Which index is "the market"?

When news reports say "the market rose", they usually mean the ASPI, because it covers every listed company. Professional investors often watch the S&P SL20 too, because its 20 companies are easier to trade in large amounts.

Can I invest in the ASPI directly?

You cannot buy an index itself. You can spread your money across many listed companies yourself, or use a unit trust that invests across the market. Check the fund’s fees and holdings before investing.

Does a rising index mean my shares went up?

Not necessarily. The ASPI is weighted by company size, so a few large companies can lift it while most smaller shares fall. Check market breadth (how many shares rose versus fell) as well.
