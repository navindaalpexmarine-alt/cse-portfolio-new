P/E and P/BV ratios explained for CSE investors

A share's price on its own says little. A Rs 20 share is not "cheaper" than a Rs 400 share. To compare companies you need to relate the price to what the business earns and what it owns. The two most used ratios for that are the price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) and the price-to-book-value ratio (P/BV).

Not investment advice. Ratios are tools for asking better questions. They do not tell you to buy or sell anything.

P/E: price ÷ earnings per share

Earnings per share (EPS) = profit attributable to ordinary shareholders ÷ number of ordinary shares.

P/E = share price ÷ EPS. It tells you how many rupees investors are paying for each rupee of yearly profit.

Example: a company earned Rs 1.2 billion over the last four quarters and has 100 million shares. EPS = Rs 12. At a price of Rs 96, P/E = 96 ÷ 12 = 8 times. Turned around, the earnings yield is 12 ÷ 96 = 12.5%.

Which earnings to use

  • Trailing (TTM): the last four quarters added together. Based on actual results.
  • Annual: the last full financial year. Can be out of date by many months.
  • Annualised quarter: one quarter × 4. Quick, but distorted by seasonal businesses.

Remove clearly one-off items (such as a gain from selling land) when judging normal earning power.

P/BV: price ÷ net asset value per share

Book value (net assets, shareholders' equity) = total assets − total liabilities, as shown in the balance sheet. Divide by the number of shares for net asset value (NAV) per share.

P/BV = share price ÷ NAV per share.

Example: equity of Rs 15 billion and 100 million shares gives NAV per share of Rs 150. At Rs 96, P/BV = 0.64: investors value the company at 64% of its stated net assets.

Linking the two: return on equity

Return on equity (ROE) = profit ÷ equity. In the example, 1.2 ÷ 15 = 8%. A useful identity: P/BV = P/E × ROE (0.64 = 8 × 0.08). Companies that earn high returns on their equity usually trade at higher P/BV; companies that earn low returns often trade below book value. So a low P/BV may simply reflect a low ROE.

Where each ratio works best

  • P/E suits companies with steady profits: consumer goods, telecoms, diversified groups.
  • P/BV suits companies whose assets are mostly financial and carried near market value: banks, finance companies and insurers. It is also used for property and investment companies.
  • Neither works well for loss-making companies (P/E is meaningless) or companies whose main value is not on the balance sheet (brands, licences).

Common traps

  1. Value traps: a low P/E can be low because profits are about to fall.
  2. One-off profits: a big gain inflates EPS and makes P/E look low for one year.
  3. Group structures: use profit attributable to the company's own shareholders, not total group profit including minority interests.
  4. Voting and non-voting shares: both share the same EPS but trade at different prices, so their P/E ratios differ.
  5. Stale data: book values from a balance sheet many months old can be out of date, especially after currency moves.

Using ratios with other information

Ratios are a starting point. Read the latest results (how to read quarterly results), look at debt levels, cash flow and dividend history, and think about the business's prospects. Frequently asked questions

What is a good P/E for a CSE share?

There is no single good number. P/E depends on the sector, interest rates, how fast profits are growing and how reliable they are. Compare a company with its own history and with similar companies, not with a fixed rule.

Is a share with P/BV below 1 always undervalued?

No. A low P/BV can mean the market doubts the stated asset values or expects poor returns on them. It is a reason to look closer, not a conclusion.

Where do I find EPS and net asset value per share?

In the company’s quarterly interim financial statements and annual report, published as CSE announcements. Many companies show EPS and net asset value per share on the summary page.
