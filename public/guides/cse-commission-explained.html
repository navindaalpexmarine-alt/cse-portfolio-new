CSE buy and sell commission explained (the 1.12% fee)

Every time you buy or sell shares on the Colombo Stock Exchange (CSE) you pay a transaction cost on top of the share price. For normal-sized trades it is 1.12% of the trade value, on both the buy and the sell. Knowing exactly how it works stops you from mistaking a small price rise for a profit.

Not investment advice. Figures below follow the CSE's published transaction cost table at the time of writing. Your contract note from your broker is always the final word.

The 1.12% in one table

For equity trades up to Rs 100 million, the CSE publishes a combined cost of 1.12%. It is not one fee but five, collected by your broker and passed on to each institution:

PartRateWho receives it
Brokerage fee0.640%Your stockbroking firm
CSE fee0.084%Colombo Stock Exchange
CDS fee0.024%Central Depository Systems (holds your shares electronically)
SEC cess0.072%Securities and Exchange Commission of Sri Lanka
Share transaction levy0.300%Government
Total1.120%

The brokerage fee is the largest part, a little more than half. The share transaction levy is a government charge that applies to both buyer and seller.

Worked example: buying

You buy 500 shares at Rs 120.00.

  • Trade value: 500 × 120.00 = Rs 60,000.00
  • Transaction cost: 60,000 × 1.12% = Rs 672.00
  • Total cash you pay: 60,000 + 672 = Rs 60,672.00

Your real cost per share is therefore 60,672 ÷ 500 = Rs 121.34, not Rs 120.00. This "cost per share including fees" is the number that matters for working out profit.

Worked example: selling

Later you sell the same 500 shares at Rs 125.00.

  • Trade value: 500 × 125.00 = Rs 62,500.00
  • Transaction cost: 62,500 × 1.12% = Rs 700.00
  • Cash you receive: 62,500 − 700 = Rs 61,800.00

Profit = 61,800 − 60,672 = Rs 1,128.00. The price rose by Rs 5.00 a share (Rs 2,500 in total), but Rs 1,372 of that went in fees across the two trades. If you had sold at Rs 122.00 instead, you would have received 61,000 − 683.20 = Rs 60,316.80, which is a loss of Rs 355.20 even though the price was "up" Rs 2.

Why a small rise is often a loss

Because you pay about 1.12% going in and 1.12% coming out, the price has to rise roughly 2.27% above your purchase price before you are even. On a Rs 100 share that is about Rs 102.27. Our guide on break-even sell price shows the exact formula. This is the single most common surprise for new investors who watch prices tick up a few cents and sell.

Large trades: the step-up above Rs 100 million

The CSE applies the fees on a step-up basis. The first Rs 100 million of a transaction is charged at 1.12%, and the part above Rs 100 million at a lower combined rate (the CSE's example uses 0.6125%, assuming a negotiated brokerage of 0.2%). For example, a Rs 102 million trade pays 1.12% on Rs 100 million (Rs 1,120,000) plus 0.6125% on Rs 2 million (Rs 12,250), a total of Rs 1,132,250. Since 31 March 2024 the brokerage on transactions of Rs 1 billion and above is negotiable. These rules only matter for very large orders; almost every retail trade pays the flat 1.12%.

Things that are not in the 1.12%

  • Bank charges for moving money to or from your broker (for example online transfer fees).
  • Fees for corporate actions, such as paying for rights shares through a bank, which follow the company's circular.
  • Tax on dividends, which is deducted by the company before the dividend reaches you (see tax on dividends and share gains).

There is no capital gains tax on profits from selling shares listed on the CSE, so the transaction cost is the main "friction" on trading profits.

How to check your contract note

After each trade your broker issues a contract note. It should show the quantity, price, gross value, each fee (or the total), and the net amount payable or receivable. A quick check:

  1. Multiply quantity by price to get the gross value.
  2. Multiply the gross value by 0.0112. The total fees on the note should be within a few rupees of this.
  3. For a buy, net payable = gross + fees. For a sale, net receivable = gross − fees.

If you split one order into many small fills at different prices, each fill is still charged at the same percentage, so the total cost is the same as one large fill at the average price.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 1.12% charged on both buying and selling?

Yes. The transaction cost is charged on the value of every trade, so a share you buy and later sell pays it twice: once on the purchase value and once on the sale value.

Can I negotiate a lower brokerage fee?

For ordinary retail trades the brokerage part is fixed by the SEC fee structure. Brokerage became negotiable only for very large transactions (Rs 1 billion and above per transaction, from 31 March 2024). The CSE, SEC, CDS fees and the share transaction levy do not change.

Why does my contract note show slightly different numbers?

Brokers round each fee separately and may show them as separate lines. Small differences of a few rupees are normal. If the gap is larger, ask your broker for the breakdown.
