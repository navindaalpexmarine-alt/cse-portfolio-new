How to calculate your break-even sell price on the CSE

Your break-even sell price is the market price at which selling your shares gives you back exactly what you paid for them, after the buying fee you already paid and the selling fee you will pay. Below this price, a sale loses money even if the price is above what you paid.

Not investment advice. Illustrative maths using the standard 1.12% CSE transaction cost. Use your real contract notes for exact numbers.

The idea in one sentence

The cash you receive from the sale must be at least the cash you spent on the purchase.

The formula

Let c be the transaction cost as a decimal (0.0112 for 1.12%).

  • Cash spent per share = buy price × (1 + c)
  • Cash received per share = sell price × (1 − c)
  • Break-even when they are equal: sell price × (1 − c) = buy price × (1 + c)

Break-even sell price = buy price × (1 + c) ÷ (1 − c)

With c = 0.0112 the factor is 1.0112 ÷ 0.9888 = 1.02265. So the break-even price is about 2.27% above the price you paid.

Worked example

You bought 1,000 shares at Rs 48.50.

  1. Cash spent: 1,000 × 48.50 × 1.0112 = Rs 49,043.20
  2. Break-even price: 48.50 × 1.02265 = Rs 49.60 (rounded up to the next price tick)
  3. Check: selling 1,000 at Rs 49.60 gives 49,600 × 0.9888 = Rs 49,044.48, just above what you spent.

If you sold at Rs 49.40, which looks like a 90-cent gain, you would receive 49,400 × 0.9888 = Rs 48,846.72, a loss of Rs 196.48.

When you bought at different prices

Most people build a position in steps. Then the right starting point is your total cash spent, not any single price.

Selling only part of a holding

If you sell some of your shares, the break-even price for the shares you keep does not change, because each share carries the same average cost. What changes is how much money you have tied up. Many investors find it useful to note the realised profit or loss on the part they sold separately, so it does not hide in the average.

Things the simple formula leaves out

  • Dividends received. Cash dividends you already got improve your overall result, but they are not part of the share's price.
  • Rights and bonus issues. After a bonus issue you own more shares at a lower cost each; after a rights issue you paid extra cash for new shares. Update your cost when these happen (see rights issues and bonus issues and splits).
  • Price ticks. Shares trade in fixed price steps, so the exact break-even may fall between two prices. Round up to be safe.
  • Very large trades above Rs 100 million use a lower stepped fee (see commission explained).

Using it in CSE Portfolio

For every holding the app shows your average cost, the break-even sell price (labelled B.E.S) and the gap between today's price and that level. The status label is purely arithmetic: at or above break-even it shows SELL, close to it NEAR, and below it WAIT. It never looks at whether a share is "cheap" or "expensive"; that judgement stays with you.

Frequently asked questions

Is break-even the same as my average price?

No. Your average price is what you paid per share. Break-even is the price you must sell at so that, after paying the selling fee, you get back everything you spent including the buying fee. With 1.12% fees it is about 2.27% higher than your average purchase price.

Do dividends change my break-even price?

A dividend you have already received is cash in your pocket, so some investors subtract it from their cost. The strict break-even price used in CSE Portfolio does not, so that it always shows the price at which the share itself pays you back.

Why does the app show SELL when the price is above break-even?

In CSE Portfolio the label only means the current price is at or above your break-even, so selling now would not lose money after fees. It is a calculation, not advice to sell.
