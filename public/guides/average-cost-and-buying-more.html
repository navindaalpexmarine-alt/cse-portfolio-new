Average cost: what happens when you buy more of a share

Few people buy all their shares in one go. Each new purchase changes your average cost per share, and with it your break-even price and your profit or loss. Understanding the arithmetic helps you see what a new purchase really does, especially when you are tempted to "average down" on a falling share.

Not investment advice. The examples illustrate arithmetic only. They are not suggestions to buy more of any share.

The formula

Average cost = total cash spent (including fees) ÷ total shares held

Each purchase adds its quantity to the total shares and its cash (price × quantity × 1.0112) to the total cost.

Example 1: buying more at a lower price (averaging down)

StepSharesPriceCash incl. 1.12%Total sharesTotal costAverage cost
Buy 11,000Rs 80.00Rs 80,8961,000Rs 80,896Rs 80.90
Buy 21,000Rs 60.00Rs 60,6722,000Rs 141,568Rs 70.78

Break-even fell from about Rs 81.81 to about Rs 71.60. The price now needs to rise 19% from Rs 60 to break even, instead of 36%. That is the appeal of averaging down.

But your money at risk also went up from about Rs 81,000 to Rs 142,000. If the price keeps falling to Rs 45, your loss is about Rs 52,500 instead of about Rs 36,000. Averaging down makes recovery easier and makes a continued fall more painful.

Example 2: buying more at a higher price (averaging up)

StepSharesPriceCash incl. 1.12%Total sharesTotal costAverage cost
Buy 1500Rs 40.00Rs 20,224500Rs 20,224Rs 40.45
Buy 2500Rs 55.00Rs 27,8081,000Rs 48,032Rs 48.03

Your profit in rupees grows if the price keeps rising, but your average cost and break-even price rise too. A small pull-back can turn a comfortable profit into a loss on the whole position.

The averaging-down trap

A falling price can mean the market has spotted a real problem: lower profits, rising debt, a coming rights issue. Buying more because "the average will be lower" focuses on your own cost, which the market does not care about. Before adding to a losing position, ask:

  1. Has anything changed in the business since I first bought? Check the latest results.
  2. Would I buy this share today if I did not already own it?
  3. After this purchase, will this one share be too large a part of my portfolio?

If the honest answers are "yes", "no" and "yes", the lower average is not a good reason.

Selling part of a holding

With the average cost method, selling some shares does not change the average cost of the rest. Example: you hold 2,000 shares at an average cost of Rs 70.78 and sell 500 at Rs 75.

  • Cash received: 500 × 75 × 0.9888 = Rs 37,080
  • Cost of shares sold: 500 × 70.784 = Rs 35,392
  • Realised profit: Rs 1,688
  • Remaining: 1,500 shares, still at Rs 70.78 average.

Corporate actions

  • Bonus issue or split: more shares, same total cost, so the average falls in proportion (details).
  • Rights taken up: treated like a purchase at the rights price (details).

Frequently asked questions

Does selling part of my holding change my average cost?

Under the average cost method used by most trackers and brokers, no. The shares you keep still carry the same average cost per share. The sale creates a realised profit or loss on the shares sold.

Why is my broker’s average price different from CSE Portfolio’s?

Some broker platforms show an average price without fees, or treat bonus issues and rights differently. CSE Portfolio includes the buying commission in your cost by default so that profit and loss reflect cash really spent.

Is buying regularly every month a good idea?

Investing a fixed amount regularly spreads your purchases over time and avoids putting everything in at one price. Whether it suits you depends on your goals and the shares chosen, and every purchase still pays the transaction cost.
