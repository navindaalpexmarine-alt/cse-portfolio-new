Bonus issues and share splits: why your shares doubled and the price halved

Sometimes you check your CDS statement and find you own twice as many shares, while the share price has halved. That is usually a bonus issue or a share split (subdivision). Your total investment is unchanged, but your quantity and cost per share must be updated, or every profit-and-loss figure goes wrong.

Not investment advice. Details of each corporate action are in the company's CSE announcement and circular.

Bonus issue (capitalisation issue)

A bonus issue gives existing shareholders free new shares in a fixed ratio, such as "1 for 10" (one new share for every ten held). The company converts part of its reserves into share capital. No money changes hands and the company's total value does not change.

A share trades XB (ex-bonus) from a set date; buyers on or after it do not receive the bonus shares, and the price adjusts down.

Share split (subdivision)

A subdivision splits each existing share into several, for example 1 into 2. It is usually done to bring a high share price down to a more convenient level for trading. The economic effect is the same as a bonus issue: more shares, lower price each, same total.

Consolidation

The opposite of a split: several shares are combined into one, raising the price per share. It is less common and usually used when a price has become very low.

Worked example: 1-for-4 bonus

You own 800 shares. Your total cost, including fees, is Rs 97,080 (average Rs 121.35). The share trades at Rs 130 before the XB date.

  • New shares: 800 ÷ 4 = 200, so you now hold 1,000 shares.
  • Total cost stays Rs 97,080, so the new average cost is Rs 97.08.
  • Theoretical price after the bonus: 130 ÷ 1.25 = Rs 104.
  • Market value before: 800 × 130 = Rs 104,000. After: 1,000 × 104 = Rs 104,000. Unchanged.
  • Break-even sell price falls in the same proportion, from about Rs 122.72 to about Rs 98.18.

Fractions

If your holding does not divide exactly by the ratio, companies usually do not issue fractions of a share. The circular explains how fractions are handled, for example by aggregating and selling them and paying the cash to shareholders, or by ignoring them.

Why companies do it

  • To make the share price more affordable and increase trading.
  • To reflect accumulated reserves in share capital.
  • To meet listing requirements on the number of shares in public hands.

None of these change what the business earns, so treat headlines about "free shares" with care.

Dividends after a bonus or split

Dividends are declared per share. After a 1-for-1 bonus, a company that used to pay Rs 4 per share would pay about Rs 2 per share to keep the total the same, and you have twice as many shares. When comparing dividend history, adjust old figures by the same factor.

Updating your records

  1. Multiply your quantity by the factor (or add the bonus shares).
  2. Keep the total cost the same.
  3. Divide the average cost by the factor.
  4. Check the new quantity against your CDS statement once the shares are credited.

Frequently asked questions

Does a bonus issue make me richer?

Not by itself. You own more shares, but each is worth proportionally less, so your total value is roughly unchanged on the day. Any later change depends on how the business performs.

Do I pay anything for bonus shares?

No. Bonus shares are issued free, by converting company reserves into share capital. A rights issue, in contrast, requires payment.

Why did my app show a huge loss after a split?

If the quantity was not updated but the price fell, the record compares the old quantity with the new lower price. Update the quantity and keep the total cost the same, and the loss disappears.
